## 1. What is This Chapter About?

This chapter deals with the **wave nature of light** — treating light as a wave rather than a particle.

Think of it this way:
- Why does a **soap bubble** show beautiful colors? → Because of **interference** of light waves.
- Why does light **bend around the corners** of obstacles? → Because of **diffraction**.
- Why do **3D glasses** work? → Because of **polarisation** of light.
- Why does a **CD/DVD** show rainbow colors? → Interference and diffraction of light.

This chapter answers all these questions scientifically.

**What you will learn:**
- Huygens' Principle (how light waves travel)
- Interference of light (Young's Double Slit Experiment)
- Diffraction of light (bending around obstacles)
- Polarisation of light (Malus's Law, Brewster's Law)

> 💡 **Key Concept:** In Chapter 9 (Ray Optics), we treated light as RAYS. In Chapter 10 (Wave Optics), we treat light as WAVES. Both are valid — it depends on the situation.

## 2. Wave Nature of Light — A Brief History

### 2.1 Historical Background

| Scientist | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Newton** | Proposed Corpuscular Theory — light consists of tiny particles called "corpuscles" |
| **Huygens** | Proposed Wave Theory — light is a wave (1678) |
| **Young** | Proved wave nature through Double Slit Experiment (1801) |
| **Maxwell** | Showed light is an electromagnetic wave (1865) |
| **Hertz** | Experimentally confirmed Maxwell's theory |
| **Planck & Einstein** | Showed light also behaves as a particle (photon) — dual nature |

### 2.2 Wavefront

**Definition:** *"A wavefront is the locus of all points in a medium which are vibrating in the same phase."*

In simple words: a wavefront is a surface where all points have the same phase (same state of vibration) at the same time.

**Types of Wavefronts:**

| Type | Shape | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Spherical Wavefront** | Sphere | From a point source |
| **Cylindrical Wavefront** | Cylinder | From a line source |
| **Plane Wavefront** | Flat plane | When source is very far away (at infinity) |

```
POINT SOURCE → Spherical waves spreading outward:

         )))
        )   )
       )  *  )       * = point source
        )   )        ) ) ) = spherical wavefronts
         )))
```

> 📝 **Key Rule:** Light rays are always **perpendicular** to the wavefront.

## 3. Huygens' Principle

### 3.1 Statement ⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 2 or 3 Marks)

**Huygens' Principle states TWO things:**

1. **Every point on a wavefront acts as a secondary source** of light, emitting tiny secondary wavelets (spherical waves) in all directions with the speed of light in that medium.

2. **The new wavefront at any later instant** is the **common tangent (envelope)** to all these secondary wavelets drawn in the forward direction.

### 3.2 Explanation

```
Original wavefront AB:

A ●——————————● B
  (each point is a secondary source)
      ↓ (after time t)
      secondary wavelets (small circles) drawn from each point
      ↓
A'●—————————————●B'  ← New wavefront (tangent to all wavelets)
```

**Why only forward wavefront?**
Huygens could not explain why backward wavelets (moving opposite to light) don't exist. This was later explained by more advanced theory. For board purposes, just remember: **we take only the forward envelope**.

### 3.3 Applications of Huygens' Principle

Using Huygens' Principle, we can derive:
- Laws of Reflection
- Laws of Refraction (Snell's Law)
- Explain interference and diffraction

> 📝 **Board Exam Note:** The statement of Huygens' principle is a 2-mark question. Learn both points clearly.

## 4. Refraction of a Plane Wave (Using Huygens' Principle)

### 4.1 Derivation of Snell's Law ⭐⭐ (5 Mark Derivation)

**Setup:** A plane wavefront AB is incident on an interface (surface separating medium 1 and medium 2). Medium 1 has speed v₁, Medium 2 has speed v₂.

**Let:**
- i = angle of incidence (angle of incident wavefront with surface)
- r = angle of refraction
- BC = distance traveled in medium 1 in time t → BC = v₁t
- AD = distance traveled in medium 2 in time t → AD = v₂t

**Derivation:**

In triangle ABC:
```
sin i = BC/AC = v₁t/AC
```

In triangle ACD:
```
sin r = AD/AC = v₂t/AC
```

Dividing:
```
sin i     v₁t/AC     v₁
—————  =  ————————  = ——
sin r     v₂t/AC     v₂
```

Since v = c/n (where n = refractive index and c = speed of light):
```
v₁ = c/n₁   and   v₂ = c/n₂
```

Therefore:
```
sin i     v₁     c/n₁     n₂
—————  =  ——  =  ————  =  ——
sin r     v₂     c/n₂     n₁
```

**This gives:**
```
n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r
```

**This is Snell's Law — derived from Huygens' Principle!** ✓

### 4.2 Key Conclusions from Refraction

- When light goes from **rarer to denser** medium (n₁ < n₂): v₂ < v₁, ray bends **toward** the normal (r < i).
- When light goes from **denser to rarer** medium (n₁ > n₂): v₂ > v₁, ray bends **away** from the normal (r > i).
- The **frequency** of light does NOT change on refraction — only speed and wavelength change.
- Relationship: **λ₁/λ₂ = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁**

## 5. Reflection of a Plane Wave (Using Huygens' Principle)

### 5.1 Proof of Laws of Reflection ⭐

**Setup:** A plane wavefront AB strikes a reflecting surface. Point A touches the surface first.

Using a similar geometric construction as refraction:

In the triangles formed:
```
sin i = BC/AC
sin r = AD/AC
```

Since both B and D are in the **same medium**, the wavelet speeds are equal:
```
BC = AD  (same medium, same time)
```

Therefore:
```
sin i = sin r
→ i = r
```

**This proves the Law of Reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection** ✓

Also, the incident ray, reflected ray, and normal all lie in the same plane — the second law of reflection is also proved.

> 📝 **Board Exam Tip:** Draw a clear diagram with wavefront, the surface, angles i and r labeled for full marks.

## 6. Coherent and Incoherent Sources

### 6.1 Coherent Sources ⭐ (Very Important for Interference)

**Definition:** *"Two sources of light are said to be coherent if they emit light waves of the same frequency (or wavelength) and have a constant phase difference."*

**Examples:**
- Two slits illuminated by the same light source (as in Young's experiment)
- A laser beam split into two beams

**Key Point:** Coherent sources produce **sustained (steady) interference pattern**.

### 6.2 Incoherent Sources

**Definition:** *"Two sources are incoherent if they emit light waves of the same frequency but with a randomly changing (non-constant) phase difference."*

**Examples:**
- Two separate bulbs (even of same type)
- Two separate sodium lamps

**Key Point:** Incoherent sources **cannot produce a steady interference pattern** — the bright and dark fringes change position so rapidly that only a uniform illumination is seen.

### 6.3 Why Can't Two Separate Bulbs Create Interference?

The atoms in a bulb emit light in random bursts (wave packets). The phase difference between two separate bulbs changes billions of times per second. The eye cannot follow such rapid changes, so only average illumination is seen — no pattern.

## 7. Interference of Light

### 7.1 What is Interference? ⭐⭐

**Definition:** *"Interference is the phenomenon of redistribution of energy when two or more coherent waves superpose (overlap) in the same region of space."*

This follows from the **Principle of Superposition:**
> The resultant displacement at any point is the algebraic sum of displacements due to individual waves at that point.

### 7.2 Types of Interference

#### Constructive Interference (Bright Fringe)
- Two waves arrive **in phase** (phase difference = 0°, 360°, 720°... or path difference = 0, λ, 2λ...).
- Crests meet crests, troughs meet troughs.
- Resultant amplitude is **maximum**.
- **Intensity is maximum.**

```
Path difference for constructive interference:
Δ = nλ     where n = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...
```

#### Destructive Interference (Dark Fringe)
- Two waves arrive **out of phase** (phase difference = 180°, 540°... or path difference = λ/2, 3λ/2...).
- Crest meets trough — they cancel.
- Resultant amplitude is **minimum (zero if amplitudes are equal)**.
- **Intensity is minimum (zero).**

```
Path difference for destructive interference:
Δ = (2n−1)λ/2     where n = 1, 2, 3, ...
  = λ/2, 3λ/2, 5λ/2, ...
```

### 7.3 Intensity Formula

If two waves have amplitudes a₁ and a₂:

```
Resultant amplitude:  A² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂ cos φ

I_max = (a₁ + a₂)²       (constructive, φ = 0)
I_min = (a₁ − a₂)²       (destructive, φ = π)
```

For **equal amplitudes** (a₁ = a₂ = a):
```
I_max = 4a²
I_min = 0
```

**Key Law: Energy is conserved in interference** — bright fringes have MORE energy, dark fringes have LESS, but the total energy is the same as without interference.

## 8. Young's Double Slit Experiment (YDSE)

### 8.1 Setup ⭐⭐⭐ (Most Important — 5 Marks)

Thomas Young (1801) demonstrated interference using two slits.

```
          S₁
         /
SOURCE →S        Screen with        Observation
         \       two slits          Screen
          S₂          |                |
                      |←——— D ————→   |
                    Slits           Fringes
```

**Parameters:**
- `d` = distance between the two slits S₁ and S₂
- `D` = distance between the slit plane and the observation screen
- `λ` = wavelength of light used
- `y` = position of a point P on the screen from the central fringe

### 8.2 Derivation of Fringe Width ⭐⭐⭐

**Path Difference:**

For a point P at height y on the screen:

Using geometry (approximation for D >> d):
```
Path difference:  Δ = S₂P − S₁P = yd/D
```

**Condition for Bright Fringe (Constructive Interference):**
```
yd/D = nλ
y_n = nλD/d     (position of n-th bright fringe)
```

where n = 0, ±1, ±2, ±3, ...
- n = 0 → Central bright fringe (at y = 0)
- n = 1 → First bright fringe, and so on.

**Condition for Dark Fringe (Destructive Interference):**
```
yd/D = (2n−1)λ/2
y_n = (2n−1)λD/2d
```

where n = 1, 2, 3, ...

**Fringe Width (β):**

Fringe width = distance between two consecutive bright (or dark) fringes:
```
β = y_(n+1) − y_n = (n+1)λD/d − nλD/d

    λD
β = ——
    d
```

**This is the most important formula of this chapter!**

### 8.3 Key Observations in YDSE

| Change | Effect on Fringe Width β |
|---|---|
| Increase D (move screen away) | β increases |
| Increase d (slits farther apart) | β decreases |
| Increase λ (use red instead of violet) | β increases |
| Immerse in medium of refractive index n | β decreases (λ becomes λ/n) |
| Use white light | Colored fringes (violet innermost, red outermost) |

### 8.4 Angular Fringe Width

```
θ = β/D = λ/d
```

### 8.5 Maximum Number of Fringes

The maximum order n is limited by the condition that y ≤ d/2 (the point must be within the geometry):
```
n_max = d/λ     (approximately)
```

### 8.6 Intensity Distribution in YDSE

For two equal sources (a₁ = a₂ = a):
```
I = 4I₀ cos²(φ/2)
```

where:
- I₀ = intensity due to one slit
- φ = phase difference = (2π/λ) × path difference = (2π/λ) × (yd/D)

- At bright fringes: φ = 0, 2π, 4π... → I = 4I₀ (maximum)
- At dark fringes: φ = π, 3π, 5π... → I = 0 (minimum)

> 📝 **Board Exam Tip:** The derivation of fringe width is a compulsory 5-mark question. Draw the diagram with S₁, S₂, P, D, d, y clearly labeled. Show every step.

## 9. Diffraction of Light

### 9.1 What is Diffraction? ⭐

**Definition:** *"Diffraction is the phenomenon of bending of light around the corners or edges of an obstacle or aperture (opening), and spreading of light into the region of geometrical shadow."*

**Simple explanation:** When light passes through a very narrow slit or around a sharp edge, it doesn't travel in a perfectly straight line — it spreads out (bends). This is diffraction.

**Condition for diffraction:** Diffraction is significant only when the size of the obstacle or opening is **comparable to the wavelength** of light (~ 10⁻⁷ m for visible light).

**Why don't we normally see diffraction of light?**  
Because ordinary objects (doors, windows) are much larger than the wavelength of light. Sound waves (wavelength ~ 1 m) diffract easily around walls — that's why you can hear someone speaking around a corner.

### 9.2 Difference from Interference

| Interference | Diffraction |
|---|---|
| Superposition of waves from TWO (or more) separate coherent sources | Superposition of wavelets from DIFFERENT parts of the SAME wavefront |
| Bright and dark fringes are of equal width | Central maximum is wider; successive maxima are narrower |
| All bright fringes have equal intensity | Central maximum is most intense; others decrease |

## 10. Single Slit Diffraction

### 10.1 Setup and Derivation ⭐⭐ (Board Exam 5 Marks)

**Setup:**
- A single slit of width `a` is illuminated by monochromatic light of wavelength `λ`.
- A screen is placed at distance `D` from the slit.
- We observe the intensity pattern on the screen.

```
        Slit        Screen
        (width a)
         |          |
  →→→→→  |          |
  →→→→→  |   →→→→   |  Central bright maximum
  →→→→→  |          |
         |          |
         ←——D——→
```

### 10.2 Condition for Minima (Dark Fringes) ⭐

The slit can be imagined as divided into pairs of halves. For a point at angle θ on the screen, each pair produces destructive interference when:

**For n-th minimum (dark fringe):**
```
a sin θ = nλ       where n = ±1, ±2, ±3, ...
```

For small angles (sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ):
```
Position of n-th minimum: y_n = nλD/a
```

**Note:** n = 0 is NOT a minimum — it's the **central maximum** (at y = 0).

### 10.3 Width of Central Maximum ⭐

The central maximum is the bright region between the first minima on either side.

- First minimum on one side: y₁ = λD/a
- First minimum on the other side: y₋₁ = −λD/a

```
Width of central maximum = 2y₁ = 2λD/a
```

**Angular width of central maximum:**
```
2θ₁ = 2λ/a
```

**Key Observations:**
- Central maximum is **twice as wide** as any secondary maximum.
- As slit width `a` increases → central maximum becomes narrower (less diffraction).
- As `a` decreases → central maximum becomes wider (more diffraction).
- When `a → ∞` (very large slit): no diffraction — we get ray optics behavior.

### 10.4 Condition for Secondary Maxima

Secondary maxima occur approximately at:
```
a sin θ = (2n+1)λ/2       where n = 1, 2, 3, ...
```

i.e., at sin θ = 3λ/2a, 5λ/2a, 7λ/2a, ...

**Intensity of secondary maxima decreases** rapidly: I₁ ≈ I₀/22, I₂ ≈ I₀/61, ...

### 10.5 Diffraction Pattern vs Interference Pattern

```
Intensity
    |
    |         SINGLE SLIT DIFFRACTION:
    |               ████
    |            ██ ████ ██
    |          ██  ██████  ██
    |_______██████████████████______
                    ↑
              Central max (widest, brightest)

Intensity
    |         DOUBLE SLIT INTERFERENCE:
    |       (equal bright fringes)
    |   ██  ██  ██  ██  ██  ██  ██
    |_________________________________
```

## 11. Difference: Interference vs Diffraction

| Feature | Interference | Diffraction |
|---|---|---|
| **Origin** | Two or more coherent sources | Single wavefront (different parts) |
| **Fringe width** | All fringes equal width | Central max is double others |
| **Fringe intensity** | All bright fringes equal | Intensity decreases from centre |
| **Dark fringes** | Perfectly dark (if equal amplitudes) | Not perfectly dark |
| **Example** | Young's double slit | Single slit pattern |

## 12. Polarisation of Light

### 12.1 What is Polarisation? ⭐⭐

**Definition:** *"Polarisation is the phenomenon in which the vibrations of light waves are restricted to a single plane."*

**Understanding polarisation:**

- Ordinary (unpolarised) light has vibrations in ALL directions perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
- **Plane polarised light** has vibrations in ONLY ONE direction (plane).

```
Unpolarised light:           Plane Polarised light:
  ↕ ↗ ↔ ↘ ↕                      |    |    |
  (vibrations in               (vibrations in
   all planes)                  ONE plane only)
```

**Key Fact:** Only **transverse waves** can be polarised. Longitudinal waves (like sound) CANNOT be polarised.

Since light CAN be polarised, this confirms that **light is a transverse wave**.

### 12.2 Methods of Polarisation

1. **By Polaroids (Polarising sheets)** — Most common in NCERT
2. **By Reflection** (Brewster's Law)
3. **By Refraction**
4. **By Scattering**

### 12.3 Polariser and Analyser ⭐

- **Polariser:** The first polaroid that converts unpolarised light into plane polarised light.
- **Analyser:** The second polaroid used to detect/analyse polarised light.

```
Unpolarised → [POLARISER] → Plane Polarised → [ANALYSER] → Observed Intensity
  light                       light
```

- When the axes of Polariser and Analyser are **parallel** (θ = 0°): Maximum intensity transmitted.
- When they are **perpendicular** (θ = 90°): Zero intensity (crossed polaroids — complete extinction).

## 13. Malus's Law

### 13.1 Statement ⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 2 to 3 Marks)

**Statement:** *"When plane polarised light is passed through an analyser, the intensity of the transmitted light varies as the square of the cosine of the angle between the transmission axes of the polariser and the analyser."*

### 13.2 Mathematical Form

```
I = I₀ cos²θ
```

Where:
- `I` = Intensity of transmitted light (through analyser)
- `I₀` = Intensity of plane polarised light incident on the analyser
- `θ` = Angle between the transmission axes of polariser and analyser

### 13.3 Special Cases

| Angle θ | I = I₀ cos²θ | Observation |
|---|---|---|
| θ = 0° | I = I₀ | Maximum intensity |
| θ = 30° | I = 3I₀/4 | Reduced intensity |
| θ = 45° | I = I₀/2 | Half intensity |
| θ = 60° | I = I₀/4 | Quarter intensity |
| θ = 90° | I = 0 | Zero intensity (complete extinction) |

### 13.4 Derivation of Malus's Law

Let the amplitude of polarised light incident on analyser be A₀. The component along the transmission axis of the analyser = A₀ cos θ.

Since Intensity ∝ (Amplitude)²:
```
I ∝ (A₀ cos θ)²
I = I₀ cos²θ
```

> 📝 **Board Exam Tip:** State the law clearly in words, then write the formula. Explain the special cases for θ = 0° and θ = 90°. This is a guaranteed 2-3 mark question.

### 13.5 What Happens with Unpolarised Light through a Polaroid?

When unpolarised light passes through a polaroid:
- The polaroid transmits only the component parallel to its axis.
- Average intensity transmitted = **I₀/2** (half the original intensity).

## 14. Brewster's Law

### 14.1 Statement ⭐⭐ (Board Exam)

**Statement:** *"When light is incident on a transparent surface at a specific angle called Brewster's angle (or polarising angle), the reflected light is completely plane polarised (vibrations only perpendicular to the plane of incidence), and the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular to each other."*

### 14.2 Brewster's Angle ⭐

At Brewster's angle (i_B), the reflected and refracted rays are at 90° to each other.

```
         Incident
           ↘  i_B
  ——————————————————  (interface)
              ↗   r = 90° − i_B
            Refracted
```

Since:
```
i_B + r = 90°   →   r = 90° − i_B
```

By Snell's law: n₁ sin i_B = n₂ sin r = n₂ sin(90° − i_B) = n₂ cos i_B

```
        sin i_B
n₂/n₁ = ——————————  =  tan i_B
        cos i_B
```

**Brewster's Law:**
```
tan i_B = n         (for air-glass interface, n₁ = 1)
```

Where:
- `i_B` = Brewster's angle (polarising angle)
- `n` = refractive index of the denser medium (glass/water)

**For glass (n ≈ 1.5):**
```
i_B = tan⁻¹(1.5) ≈ 56°
```

### 14.3 Key Points about Brewster's Law

1. Only the **reflected ray** is completely polarised (vibrations perpendicular to the plane of incidence).
2. The **refracted ray** is only **partially polarised**.
3. At Brewster's angle, the reflected and refracted rays are **perpendicular** to each other.
4. For water (n ≈ 1.33): i_B ≈ 53°.
5. This phenomenon explains why glare from roads/water at certain angles is polarised — anti-glare sunglasses use polaroids to block this.

> 📝 **Board Exam Note:** Learn the derivation of Brewster's Law (it's elegant and short). The key condition is: reflected ray ⊥ refracted ray, giving tan i_B = n.

## 16. Board Exam Questions with Answers

### 📝 1-Mark / Very Short Answer Questions

**Q1. What is a wavefront?**
**Ans:** A wavefront is the locus of all points in a medium which are vibrating in the same phase.

**Q2. State Huygens' Principle.**
**Ans:** Every point on a wavefront acts as a secondary source of wavelets. The new wavefront is the forward envelope of all these secondary wavelets.

**Q3. What is the fringe width in Young's Double Slit Experiment?**
**Ans:** β = λD/d, where λ = wavelength, D = distance to screen, d = slit separation.

**Q4. What is diffraction of light?**
**Ans:** Diffraction is the bending of light around the edges of an obstacle or opening, and its spreading into the region of geometrical shadow.

**Q5. State Malus's Law.**
**Ans:** I = I₀ cos²θ — the intensity of light transmitted through an analyser varies as the square of the cosine of the angle between polariser and analyser axes.

**Q6. What is Brewster's angle?**
**Ans:** It is the angle of incidence at which the reflected ray is completely plane polarised. tan i_B = n.

**Q7. What is the condition for constructive interference?**
**Ans:** Path difference Δ = nλ, where n = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...

**Q8. What is a polaroid?**
**Ans:** A polaroid is a thin sheet of special material that polarises light by transmitting only those vibrations which are parallel to its transmission axis.

**Q9. Why can't two separate sodium lamps produce interference?**
**Ans:** Two separate lamps are incoherent sources — they have a randomly changing phase difference, so no sustained interference pattern is produced.

**Q10. What happens to fringe width if a YDSE is immersed in water (n = 4/3)?**
**Ans:** Fringe width decreases. β' = β/n = 3β/4 (wavelength becomes λ/n in water).

### 📝 2-Mark Questions

**Q11. Derive the condition for destructive interference.**

**Ans:** For destructive interference, two waves must be out of phase by π (180°), so the path difference must be an odd multiple of λ/2:
```
Δ = (2n−1)λ/2     n = 1, 2, 3, ...
i.e., Δ = λ/2, 3λ/2, 5λ/2, ...
```
The waves cancel, giving zero (or minimum) intensity.

---

**Q12. What are coherent sources? Why are they necessary for interference?**

**Ans:** Coherent sources are those which emit light of the same frequency with a constant phase difference. They are necessary because if the phase difference changes randomly, the positions of bright and dark fringes shift rapidly, and only uniform illumination is seen (no sustained pattern).

---

**Q13. State and explain Malus's Law with a diagram.**

**Ans:**
Statement: I = I₀ cos²θ

When plane polarised light of intensity I₀ passes through an analyser, if the angle between the transmission axes is θ, the transmitted intensity is I = I₀ cos²θ.

- θ = 0° → I = I₀ (maximum)
- θ = 90° → I = 0 (zero — crossed polaroids)

**Q14. What is the width of the central maximum in a single slit diffraction pattern?**

**Ans:**
```
Width of central maximum = 2λD/a
```
Where a = slit width, D = distance to screen, λ = wavelength.

As slit width `a` increases, central maximum becomes narrower. For very large `a`, diffraction disappears.

### 📝 3-Mark Questions

**Q15. State and prove Brewster's Law.**

**Ans:**

**Statement:** At Brewster's angle (i_B), tan i_B = n (refractive index of denser medium).

**Proof:**
At Brewster's angle, the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular:
```
i_B + r = 90°  →  r = 90° − i_B
```

By Snell's law:
```
n₁ sin i_B = n₂ sin r
sin i_B = n sin(90° − i_B) = n cos i_B
sin i_B / cos i_B = n
tan i_B = n
```
**Hence Brewster's Law is proved.** ✓

**Q16. Explain the difference between interference and diffraction (give 3 points).**

**Ans:**

| Interference | Diffraction |
|---|---|
| Superposition of waves from two or more separate sources | Superposition of wavelets from different parts of SAME wavefront |
| All bright fringes are of equal width and intensity | Central maximum is wider and brighter than secondary maxima |
| Dark fringes are perfectly dark (equal amplitudes) | Dark fringes are not perfectly dark |

---

**Q17. In YDSE, what changes if: (a) slit separation is doubled, (b) wavelength is doubled, (c) screen distance is halved?**

**Ans:** Using β = λD/d:
- (a) d doubled → β halved (β/2). Fringes become closer.
- (b) λ doubled → β doubled (2β). Fringes become wider.
- (c) D halved → β halved (β/2). Fringes become closer.

### 📝 5-Mark Questions (Long Answer)

**Q18. Derive the expression for fringe width in Young's Double Slit Experiment.**

**Ans:** *(See Section 8.2 — write the full derivation with diagram)*

Steps:
1. Draw the setup with S₁, S₂ (separation d), screen at distance D, point P at height y.
2. Path difference: Δ = yd/D (derive using geometry).
3. For bright fringe: Δ = nλ → y_n = nλD/d.
4. Fringe width: β = y_(n+1) − y_n = λD/d.

**Final Answer: β = λD/d**

---

**Q19. Derive Snell's Law using Huygens' Wave Theory.**

**Ans:** *(See Section 4.1 — write the full derivation with wavefront diagram)*

Key steps:
1. Draw incident wavefront AB, refracted wavefront CD.
2. Show BC = v₁t (in medium 1), AD = v₂t (in medium 2).
3. In triangles: sin i = v₁t/AC, sin r = v₂t/AC.
4. Divide: sin i/sin r = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁.
5. Therefore: n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r. ✓

---

**Q20. What is polarisation? Explain how polaroids work. State and verify Malus's Law.**

**Ans:**

**Polarisation:** Restriction of vibrations of light to one plane. Light is transverse, so only one direction of vibration is transmitted by a polaroid.

**Working of Polaroid:** A polaroid has a preferred direction (transmission axis). It absorbs the component of light perpendicular to this axis and transmits only the parallel component.

**Malus's Law:** I = I₀ cos²θ

**Verification:**
- Take two polaroids. Rotate one from 0° to 90°.
- At θ = 0°: maximum light through (bright).
- At θ = 45°: I = I₀/2.
- At θ = 90°: no light through (dark).
This confirms I = I₀ cos²θ. ✓

### 📝 Numerical Problems (Board Pattern)

**Num 1:** In YDSE, slits are 0.2 mm apart, screen is 1 m away, and wavelength of light = 600 nm. Find the fringe width.

**Solution:**
```
Given: d = 0.2 mm = 0.2×10⁻³ m, D = 1 m, λ = 600 nm = 600×10⁻⁹ m

β = λD/d
  = (600×10⁻⁹ × 1) / (0.2×10⁻³)
  = 600×10⁻⁹ / 2×10⁻⁴
  = 3×10⁻³ m = 3 mm
```
**Answer: β = 3 mm**

---

**Num 2:** Two slits in YDSE are 0.5 mm apart. Fringes are observed on a screen 1.5 m away. If 10th bright fringe is 1.5 cm from centre, find wavelength.

**Solution:**
```
y₁₀ = 10λD/d
1.5×10⁻² = 10 × λ × 1.5 / (0.5×10⁻³)
λ = (1.5×10⁻² × 0.5×10⁻³) / (10 × 1.5)
  = (7.5×10⁻⁶) / 15
  = 5×10⁻⁷ m = 500 nm
```
**Answer: λ = 500 nm (green light)**

---

**Num 3:** The refractive index of glass is 1.73. Find Brewster's angle.

**Solution:**
```
tan i_B = n = 1.73
i_B = tan⁻¹(1.73) = 60°
```
**Answer: i_B = 60°**

---

**Num 4:** Polarised light of intensity I₀ is incident on an analyser. The transmitted intensity is I₀/4. Find the angle between polariser and analyser.

**Solution:**
```
I = I₀ cos²θ
I₀/4 = I₀ cos²θ
cos²θ = 1/4
cos θ = 1/2
θ = 60°
```
**Answer: θ = 60°**

---

**Num 5:** In single slit diffraction, wavelength = 500 nm, slit width = 0.1 mm, screen at 1 m. Find width of central maximum.

**Solution:**
```
Width = 2λD/a
      = 2 × 500×10⁻⁹ × 1 / (0.1×10⁻³)
      = 1000×10⁻⁹ / 10⁻⁴
      = 10⁻⁵ m ... wait, recalculate:
      = (2 × 500×10⁻⁹ × 1) / (0.1×10⁻³)
      = 10⁻⁶ / 10⁻⁴
      = 10⁻² m = 1 cm
```
**Answer: Width = 1 cm**

---

**Num 6:** Light of intensity I₀ falls on a polaroid. Find the intensity of emerging light.

**Solution:**
```
Unpolarised light passing through a polaroid transmits only half the intensity:
I = I₀/2
```
**Answer: I = I₀/2**

### 💡 Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **YDSE path difference formula:** Δ = yd/D, NOT yD/d. (d is in numerator, D in denominator for path difference; it's opposite for fringe width β = λD/d.)
2. **Single slit minima vs maxima:** At y = 0, it is the CENTRAL MAXIMUM, not a minimum. Minima occur at a sinθ = nλ (n ≠ 0).
3. **Malus's Law:** I = I₀ cos²θ applies to PLANE POLARISED light. For unpolarised light through polaroid, I = I₀/2 (regardless of angle).
4. **Brewster's angle:** At i_B, reflected ray is FULLY polarised, refracted ray is only PARTIALLY polarised.
5. **Fringe width changes in medium:** When placed in medium of refractive index n, β' = β/n (wavelength decreases, so fringes get closer).
6. **Coherence is essential for interference:** Two ordinary bulbs CANNOT produce interference — always mention coherent sources.
7. **Width of central max in diffraction is TWICE the secondary maxima** — don't confuse the two.
