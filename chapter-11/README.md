## 1. What is This Chapter About?

This chapter is about one of the most **revolutionary ideas in modern physics** — that light behaves like a particle sometimes, and matter behaves like a wave sometimes.

Think of it this way:
- A **solar cell** converts light into electricity. How? → Light hits the metal and knocks out electrons — **Photoelectric Effect**.
- An **electron microscope** uses electrons instead of light — because electrons have wavelength! → **de Broglie's Wave-Particle Duality**.
- Why do electrons not fall into the nucleus of an atom? → Because of **wave nature of matter**.
- How do **night-vision cameras** and **photomultiplier tubes** work? → Photoelectric effect.

**What you will learn:**
- How electrons are emitted from metals (various methods)
- The photoelectric effect and Einstein's explanation
- Why wave theory of light FAILED to explain photoelectric effect
- What a photon is and its properties
- de Broglie's hypothesis — matter has wave nature
- Davisson-Germer experiment — proof of matter waves

> 💡 **The Big Idea:** Light is BOTH a wave AND a particle. Matter (electrons, protons) is ALSO both a particle AND a wave. This is called **Wave-Particle Duality**.

## 2. Electron Emission

### 2.1 What is Electron Emission?

Electrons inside a metal are free to move within it, but they are bound to the metal surface by attractive forces of the positive ions. To escape from the surface, an electron must be given sufficient energy to overcome this binding.

**The minimum energy required to emit an electron from a metal surface is called the Work Function (φ or W).**

- **Symbol:** φ (phi) or W
- **Unit:** electron volt (eV) or Joule (J)
- **1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J**

### 2.2 Methods of Electron Emission

There are four methods by which electrons can be emitted from a metal surface:

#### 1. Thermionic Emission
- Metal is **heated to a high temperature**.
- Thermal energy provides electrons sufficient energy to escape.
- **Example:** Cathode in a vacuum tube, filament in a CRT.

#### 2. Field Emission (Cold Emission)
- A **very strong electric field** (~10⁸ V/m) is applied near the metal surface.
- The field pulls out electrons even at room temperature.
- **Example:** Field emission displays.

#### 3. Photoelectric Emission ⭐ (Most Important)
- **Light of sufficient frequency** falls on the metal surface.
- Photons transfer energy to electrons, which escape from the surface.
- This is the main topic of this chapter.

#### 4. Secondary Emission
- **Fast-moving electrons (primary electrons)** strike a metal surface.
- The impact causes other electrons (secondary electrons) to be emitted.
- **Example:** Photomultiplier tubes.

### 2.3 Work Function of Common Metals

| Metal | Work Function (eV) |
|---|---|
| Cesium (Cs) | 2.14 |
| Potassium (K) | 2.30 |
| Sodium (Na) | 2.75 |
| Calcium (Ca) | 3.20 |
| Zinc (Zn) | 3.63 |
| Silver (Ag) | 4.73 |
| Platinum (Pt) | 5.65 |

> 📝 **Note:** Alkali metals (Na, K, Cs) have low work functions — they emit electrons even with visible light. This is why they are used in photoelectric cells.

## 3. Photoelectric Effect

### 3.1 Definition ⭐⭐

**Definition:** *"The photoelectric effect is the phenomenon of emission of electrons from the surface of a metal when light of suitable frequency falls on it."*

- The emitted electrons are called **photoelectrons**.
- The current produced is called **photocurrent**.

### 3.2 Historical Background

| Scientist | Year | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Heinrich Hertz** | 1887 | First observed photoelectric effect (UV light on zinc) |
| **Wilhelm Hallwachs** | 1888 | Showed negatively charged zinc plate loses charge when UV falls on it |
| **Philipp Lenard** | 1900 | Studied effect of intensity and frequency; found kinetic energy depends on frequency |
| **Albert Einstein** | 1905 | Gave the correct theoretical explanation using photons (won Nobel Prize 1921) |

### 3.3 Basic Setup

```
                    Light (hν)
                       ↓↓↓
                  ___________
         Anode   |           |   Collector
           (+)   |   METAL   |     plate
                 |___________|
                       |
                   Electrons
                  (photoelectrons)
                       |
           ←——— Galvanometer ———→
                (measures current)
```

- Light falls on the **emitter plate** (photosensitive metal).
- Electrons are emitted and collected by the **collector plate**.
- The current is measured by a **galvanometer**.
- A **battery** can be used to accelerate (positive potential) or retard (negative potential) the electrons.

## 4. Experimental Study of Photoelectric Effect

### 4.1 Effect of Intensity of Light ⭐

**Experiment:** Keep frequency constant, vary intensity.

**Observation:**
- As intensity increases → **photocurrent increases** (more electrons emitted).
- Stopping potential does **NOT** change with intensity.

**Conclusion:** The number of photoelectrons emitted is directly proportional to the intensity of light. But the maximum kinetic energy of electrons is INDEPENDENT of intensity.

### 4.2 Effect of Frequency of Light ⭐⭐

**Experiment:** Keep intensity constant, vary frequency.

**Observation:**
- Below a certain frequency (threshold frequency ν₀): **NO emission**, however intense the light.
- Above threshold frequency: emission occurs.
- As frequency increases → **stopping potential increases** (maximum KE of electrons increases).

**Conclusion:** Photoelectric effect occurs only if frequency ≥ threshold frequency (ν₀). Maximum KE depends on frequency, NOT intensity.

### 4.3 Stopping Potential (V₀) ⭐⭐

**Definition:** *"The minimum negative potential applied to the collector plate (anode) which just stops the photocurrent is called the stopping potential."*

At stopping potential, the fastest electrons are just stopped:
```
eV₀ = (1/2)mv²_max = KE_max

→ KE_max = eV₀
```

**Key Facts:**
- Stopping potential is INDEPENDENT of intensity.
- Stopping potential INCREASES with increase in frequency.
- Stopping potential depends on the nature of the emitter surface.

### 4.4 Saturation Current

- When positive potential on the collector is increased, photocurrent increases.
- Beyond a certain positive potential, photocurrent becomes constant — this is the **saturation current**.
- Saturation current increases with intensity of light.

### 4.5 Graph: Photocurrent vs Potential ⭐

```
Photocurrent
     |         Intensity I₂ (I₂ > I₁)
     |    ___________________________  ← Saturation current (I₂)
     |   /
     |  /       Intensity I₁
     | /   ________________________  ← Saturation current (I₁)
     |/   /
     |   /
     |  /
—————|—/—————————|——————————————→  Potential (V)
     |/           |
     |       −V₀ (stopping potential — SAME for both intensities)
```

**Note:** Both intensities have the SAME stopping potential (−V₀), showing KE_max is independent of intensity.

### 4.6 Graph: Stopping Potential vs Frequency ⭐⭐

```

potential
(V₀)      /
          /
         /
        /
       /
      /
—————/————→  Frequency (ν)

```

- Linear relationship between V₀ and ν.
- Intercept on x-axis = threshold frequency ν₀.
- Slope = h/e (ratio of Planck's constant to electron charge).

## 5. Laws of Photoelectric Effect

### 5.1 The Four Laws ⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 3 Marks)

**Law 1:** For a given metal, there exists a minimum frequency called the **threshold frequency (ν₀)**. Photoelectric emission occurs ONLY if the frequency of incident light is greater than or equal to ν₀.

**Law 2:** The **maximum kinetic energy** of photoelectrons depends only on the **frequency** of incident light. It is INDEPENDENT of the intensity.
```
KE_max = h(ν − ν₀)
```

**Law 3:** The **number of photoelectrons** emitted per second (photocurrent) is directly proportional to the **intensity** of incident light (provided frequency > ν₀).

**Law 4:** The photoelectric emission is an **instantaneous process** — there is no time delay between the incidence of light and the emission of electrons (delay is less than 10⁻⁹ seconds).

### 5.2 Threshold Frequency and Threshold Wavelength

- **Threshold Frequency (ν₀):** Minimum frequency below which photoelectric effect does NOT occur.
- **Threshold Wavelength (λ₀):** Maximum wavelength above which photoelectric effect does NOT occur.

```
ν₀ = φ/h         (threshold frequency)
λ₀ = c/ν₀ = hc/φ  (threshold wavelength)
```

Where φ = work function, h = Planck's constant, c = speed of light.

> 📝 **Board Exam Tip:** These four laws are the heart of the chapter. Learn them word-for-word. They come as a 3-mark question frequently.

## 6. Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

### 6.1 Einstein's Quantum Theory of Light ⭐⭐⭐ (Most Important)

Einstein (1905) proposed:
- Light consists of **packets of energy** called **quanta** or **photons**.
- Each photon carries energy: **E = hν**
- When a photon hits a metal surface:
  - Part of the energy (= φ, work function) is used to free the electron from the metal.
  - The remaining energy appears as the kinetic energy of the emitted electron.

### 6.2 Einstein's Photoelectric Equation ⭐⭐⭐

```
hν = φ + KE_max

KE_max = hν − φ

(1/2)mv²_max = hν − hν₀ = h(ν − ν₀)
```

Also, since KE_max = eV₀:
```
eV₀ = hν − φ = h(ν − ν₀)
```

Where:
- `h` = Planck's constant = **6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s**
- `ν` = frequency of incident light
- `φ` = work function of metal
- `ν₀` = threshold frequency
- `KE_max` = maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons
- `V₀` = stopping potential
- `e` = charge of electron = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

### 6.3 Explanation of All Laws Using Einstein's Equation

| Law | Explained by Einstein's Equation |
|---|---|
| Threshold frequency exists | If hν < φ, then KE_max < 0 — impossible, so no emission. ν must be ≥ ν₀ = φ/h |
| KE depends on frequency, not intensity | KE_max = hν − φ — only ν appears, not intensity |
| Photocurrent ∝ intensity | More intensity = more photons = more electrons emitted |
| Instantaneous process | One photon interacts with one electron — no energy accumulation needed |

### 6.4 Graph: KE_max vs Frequency ⭐⭐

From eV₀ = hν − φ → V₀ = (h/e)ν − φ/e

This is a straight line (y = mx + c) with:
- **Slope = h/e** (same for all metals — universal constant)
- **X-intercept = ν₀** (threshold frequency, different for each metal)
- **Y-intercept = −φ/e** (negative, different for each metal)

```
KE_max
(or V₀)
         /  Metal A    / Metal B
        /             /
       /             /  (same slope = h/e for all metals)
      /             /
—————/—————————————/————————→ frequency ν
    /             /
  ν₀A          ν₀B
```

## 7. Failure of Wave Theory to Explain Photoelectric Effect

### 7.1 The Wave Theory Predictions vs Observations ⭐⭐ (3 Mark Question)

Classical wave theory predicted the following — but experiments showed something completely different:

**Problem 1: Threshold Frequency**

- **Wave theory:** Any frequency of light, if intense enough, should eject electrons (energy builds up over time).
- **Observation:** Below threshold frequency ν₀, NO emission, no matter how intense the light.
- **Wave theory FAILS** to explain threshold frequency.

**Problem 2: KE depends on Frequency, not Intensity**

- **Wave theory:** Brighter light = more energy = faster electrons. KE should increase with intensity.
- **Observation:** KE_max is independent of intensity. It increases only with frequency.
- **Wave theory FAILS** to explain this.

**Problem 3: Instantaneous Emission**

- **Wave theory:** Energy is spread over a large wavefront. It should take minutes to hours for an electron to accumulate enough energy to escape.
- **Observation:** Emission is instantaneous (< 10⁻⁹ s), even in very weak light.
- **Wave theory FAILS** to explain instantaneous emission.

### 7.2 How Einstein's Photon Theory Succeeds

All three problems are solved by Einstein's concept of photons:
- A single photon carries concentrated energy hν.
- If hν ≥ φ, immediate emission.
- If hν < φ (low frequency), no emission — regardless of how many photons (intensity).
- More photons = more electrons, but each photon gives same energy per electron.

## 8. Photon — Nature and Properties

### 8.1 What is a Photon? ⭐

A **photon** is a quantum (packet) of electromagnetic energy. Light consists of photons.

### 8.2 Properties of a Photon ⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 3 Marks)

1. **Energy:** Each photon has energy E = hν = hc/λ

2. **Rest mass:** A photon has **zero rest mass** (m₀ = 0). But it has relativistic mass.

3. **Momentum:** Despite zero rest mass, a photon has momentum:
   ```
   p = E/c = hν/c = h/λ
   ```

4. **Speed:** A photon always travels at the speed of light c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum. It cannot be at rest.

5. **Charge:** A photon is **electrically neutral** (no charge).

6. **Electric and magnetic fields:** Photons are NOT deflected by electric or magnetic fields.

7. **Indivisible:** A photon cannot be split. In photoelectric effect, one photon interacts with one electron.

8. **Total energy of radiation:**
   ```
   Total energy = nhν    (where n = number of photons)
   ```

### 8.3 Photon Momentum and Radiation Pressure

From Einstein's mass-energy equivalence:
```
E = mc²   and   E = hν
→  m = hν/c²

Momentum p = mc = hν/c = h/λ
```

This momentum of photons causes **radiation pressure** — light exerts pressure on surfaces it falls on.

## 9. de Broglie Hypothesis — Wave Nature of Matter

### 9.1 de Broglie's Proposal ⭐⭐⭐ (Most Important — 5 Marks)

Louis de Broglie (1924) made a bold hypothesis:

**"If radiation (light) which is classically a wave can exhibit particle-like properties (photons), then particles of matter (electrons, protons, etc.) which are classically particles should also exhibit wave-like properties."**

This is the principle of **symmetry in nature**.

### 9.2 de Broglie Wavelength ⭐⭐⭐

de Broglie proposed that every moving particle has an associated wavelength called the **de Broglie wavelength (λ)**:

```
      h
λ = ——————
      mv
```

Where:
- `λ` = de Broglie wavelength (m)
- `h` = Planck's constant = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
- `m` = mass of the particle (kg)
- `v` = velocity of the particle (m/s)
- `mv` = p = momentum of the particle

**Also written as:**
```
λ = h/p      (where p = momentum)
```

### 9.3 Derivation of de Broglie Relation ⭐⭐

**From Einstein's photon theory:**
```
Energy of photon: E = hν = hc/λ
Momentum of photon: p = E/c = hν/c = h/λ
→ λ = h/p
```

de Broglie proposed this same relation for material particles:
```
λ = h/p = h/mv
```

**This is the de Broglie relation.** ✓

### 9.4 Key Points about de Broglie Waves

1. de Broglie waves are NOT electromagnetic waves — they are **matter waves** or **probability waves**.
2. Heavier the particle (larger m), **smaller** the wavelength.
3. Faster the particle (larger v), **smaller** the wavelength.
4. For macroscopic objects (cricket ball, car), λ is so tiny (~10⁻³⁴ m) that wave nature is not observable.
5. For electrons, λ is of the order of X-rays (~10⁻¹⁰ m) — observable using crystal diffraction.

> 📝 **Board Exam Tip:** The derivation of λ = h/mv using photon momentum analogy is a common 3-5 mark question. Write it clearly and state the de Broglie relation.

## 10. de Broglie Wavelength — Various Expressions

### 10.1 In Terms of Kinetic Energy ⭐⭐

If a particle has kinetic energy KE:
```
KE = (1/2)mv²  →  mv² = 2KE  →  (mv)² = 2m(KE)
p = mv = √(2m·KE)

        h
λ = ——————————
      √(2m·KE)
```

### 10.2 For Electrons Accelerated Through Potential V ⭐⭐⭐

When an electron (charge e, mass m) is accelerated through potential difference V:
```
Energy gained = eV = KE = (1/2)mv²
p = mv = √(2meV)

         h
λ = ————————————
      √(2meV)
```

**Substituting values** (h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴, m = 9.1×10⁻³¹, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹):

```
         1.227
λ = ————————————  nm
         √V
```

Or:
```
λ = 1.227/√V  nm      (V in volts, λ in nm)

More precisely:
λ = 12.27/√V  Å       (V in volts, λ in Angstroms)
```

**Example:** For V = 100 V:
```
λ = 1.227/√100 = 1.227/10 = 0.1227 nm = 1.227 Å
```

This is in the X-ray range — which is why electrons diffract from crystals!

### 10.3 For a Particle at Temperature T (Thermal de Broglie Wavelength)

At thermal equilibrium, average kinetic energy = (3/2)k_BT:
```
        h
λ = ————————————
      √(3mk_BT)
```

Where k_B = Boltzmann's constant = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K.

### 10.4 Comparison: de Broglie Wavelengths

| Particle | Mass | Speed | λ (approximate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cricket ball (0.15 kg) | 0.15 kg | 30 m/s | ~10⁻³⁴ m (undetectable) |
| Electron (100 eV) | 9.1×10⁻³¹ kg | — | ~1.2 Å (X-ray range) |
| Proton (100 eV) | 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg | — | ~0.03 Å |
| Neutron (thermal) | 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg | ~2200 m/s | ~1.8 Å |

**Why can't we observe wave nature of large objects?**
For a cricket ball, λ ~ 10⁻³⁴ m, which is far smaller than any physical measurement we can make. Wave nature becomes observable only for atomic and subatomic particles.

## 11. Davisson and Germer Experiment

### 11.1 Purpose ⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 3 to 5 Marks)

The Davisson-Germer experiment (1927) provided the **first experimental proof** of de Broglie's hypothesis that electrons (material particles) have wave nature.

**Key idea:** If electrons have wave nature, they should show **diffraction** like X-rays when scattered from a crystal (since crystal spacing ~ 10⁻¹⁰ m is comparable to electron wavelength).

### 11.2 Experimental Setup ⭐⭐

```
                     Electron Gun
                      (heated filament)
                           ↓
                      Accelerating
                       voltage V
                           ↓
                     Electron beam
                           ↓
                   ————————————————
                  |   Nickel Crystal |
                   ————————————————
                           ↓ (scattered electrons)
                     Electron Detector
                    (can rotate around)
                           ↓
                      Galvanometer
```

**Components:**
- **Electron gun:** Heated filament produces electrons, accelerated through potential V.
- **Nickel crystal:** Acts as a diffraction grating (natural crystal, like a 3D grating).
- **Electron detector:** Measures intensity of scattered electrons at various angles.
- **Galvanometer:** Measures the electron current.

### 11.3 Observation ⭐⭐

- Electrons were scattered from the nickel crystal in all directions.
- When accelerating voltage was **54 V**, a **sharp peak** in scattered electron intensity was observed at **scattering angle = 50°** (i.e., at angle 50° from the incident beam direction).
- This is analogous to Bragg diffraction of X-rays.

### 11.4 Verification ⭐⭐

**de Broglie wavelength (theoretical):** Using λ = 12.27/√V Å:
```
λ = 12.27/√54 = 12.27/7.35 = 1.67 Å
```

**Wavelength from diffraction (experimental):** Using Bragg's condition and the known spacing of nickel crystal planes (d = 0.91 Å), and the observed angle:
```
λ_exp ≈ 1.65 Å
```

**The two values are in excellent agreement!** (1.67 Å ≈ 1.65 Å)

This confirms that **electrons indeed have wave nature** — de Broglie's hypothesis is experimentally verified. ✓

### 11.5 Significance

- First experimental proof of matter waves.
- Opened the door to electron microscopy.
- Confirmed quantum mechanics' wave-particle duality.
- Davisson and Germer received the Nobel Prize (1937) along with G.P. Thomson (who independently confirmed matter waves).

> 📝 **Board Exam Tip:** Draw the setup diagram clearly — Electron gun → Nickel crystal → Detector. State the observation (50° at 54 V) and verification (λ_theory = 1.67 Å, λ_exp = 1.65 Å).

## 12. Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

### 12.1 Statement ⭐ (Mentioned in NCERT — 1 to 2 Marks)

**Statement:** *"It is impossible to simultaneously determine both the exact position and exact momentum (or velocity) of a particle with perfect accuracy. The more precisely one is known, the less precisely the other can be known."*

```
Δx × Δp ≥ h/(4π) = ħ/2

or

Δx × Δ(mv) ≥ h/(4π)
```

Where:
- `Δx` = uncertainty in position
- `Δp` = uncertainty in momentum
- `h` = Planck's constant
- `ħ = h/2π` (h-bar)

**Also for Energy-Time uncertainty:**
```
ΔE × Δt ≥ h/(4π)
```

### 12.2 Physical Meaning

- To observe an electron, we need to "illuminate" it with a photon.
- The photon disturbs the electron's momentum (recoil).
- A shorter wavelength photon (more precise position) has higher momentum and disturbs the electron MORE.
- A longer wavelength photon (less precise position) disturbs the electron LESS.
- There is a fundamental trade-off — this is NOT a limitation of our instruments, it is a **fundamental law of nature**.

### 12.3 Consequences

- Electrons cannot exist inside the nucleus (if confined to nuclear size ~10⁻¹⁴ m, their momentum uncertainty would require kinetic energy ~ 200 MeV, much larger than nuclear binding energies).
- The ground state energy of hydrogen atom is non-zero.
- This principle is the basis of the probabilistic interpretation of quantum mechanics.

> 📝 **Board Exam Note:** In Bihar Board, this is usually a 1-2 mark question — state the principle and write the formula.

```
  = 39.756×10⁻²⁰ J
  = 3.97×10⁻¹⁹ J

In eV: E = 3.97×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 2.48 eV
```
**Answer: E ≈ 2.48 eV**

---

**Q16. Why cannot wave theory explain the instantaneous nature of photoelectric emission?**

**Ans:** According to wave theory, energy is spread over the entire wavefront. A small surface area of a metal would receive very little energy per second, and it would take minutes or hours for an electron to accumulate enough energy to escape. But experimentally, emission occurs within 10⁻⁹ seconds. Einstein's photon theory explains this: a single photon interacts with a single electron and transfers all its energy instantly.

### 📝 3-Mark Questions

**Q17. Explain why photoelectric effect cannot be explained by wave theory. (3 points)**

**Ans:** *(See Section 7 — summarize the three failures)*

1. **Threshold frequency:** Wave theory predicts any frequency should work if intense enough, but experiments show no emission below ν₀.
2. **KE independent of intensity:** Wave theory predicts KE should increase with intensity, but observations show KE depends only on frequency.
3. **Instantaneous emission:** Wave theory predicts a time delay for energy accumulation, but emission is instantaneous.

---

**Q18. Derive de Broglie wavelength for an electron accelerated through potential V.**

**Ans:**

Energy gained by electron: KE = eV

Since KE = p²/2m → p = √(2m·eV)

de Broglie wavelength:
```
λ = h/p = h/√(2meV)

Substituting h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴, m = 9.1×10⁻³¹, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹:

λ = 12.27/√V  Å     (V in volts)
```

---

**Q19. State the observations and conclusions of the Davisson-Germer experiment.**

**Ans:**

**Observation:** When electrons accelerated through 54 V were scattered from a nickel crystal, a sharp intensity maximum was found at a scattering angle of 50°.

**Verification:**
- Theoretical λ (de Broglie): λ = 12.27/√54 = 1.67 Å
- Experimental λ (from diffraction pattern): λ ≈ 1.65 Å

**Conclusion:** The excellent agreement proves that electrons have wave nature — de Broglie's hypothesis is confirmed. Electrons diffract just like X-rays.

### 📝 5-Mark Questions (Long Answer)

**Q20. State and explain Einstein's photoelectric equation. How does it explain the laws of photoelectric effect?**

**Ans:**

**Einstein's Photoelectric Equation:**
```
KE_max = hν − φ = h(ν − ν₀)
eV₀ = hν − φ
```

**Derivation:** A photon of energy hν hits the metal. Minimum energy φ is used to overcome the surface binding force (work function). The remaining energy appears as kinetic energy of the emitted electron.

**Explanation of Laws:**

1. **Threshold frequency:** If hν < φ → KE_max < 0 → impossible. So emission requires hν ≥ φ → ν ≥ φ/h = ν₀. ✓

2. **KE depends on frequency:** From KE_max = hν − φ, KE depends only on ν, not on intensity. ✓

3. **Photocurrent ∝ intensity:** More intensity = more photons = more electrons emitted = more photocurrent. ✓

4. **Instantaneous:** One photon → one electron, direct energy transfer, no accumulation needed. ✓

**All four laws are explained by Einstein's equation.** ✓

---

**Q21. Explain de Broglie's hypothesis. Derive the expression λ = h/mv and discuss its significance.**

**Ans:**

**Hypothesis:** de Broglie proposed (1924) that since light (wave) shows particle nature (photons), material particles (electrons, protons) should also show wave nature.

**Derivation:**
For a photon: E = hν = hc/λ
Momentum: p = E/c = hν/c = h/λ → λ = h/p

By analogy, for a particle of mass m and velocity v:
```
λ = h/p = h/mv
```

**Significance:**
1. Explains the Bohr model's condition for allowed orbits: the electron wave must form a standing wave around the nucleus — only integer wavelengths allowed (2πr = nλ), giving Bohr's quantum condition mvr = nh/2π. ✓
2. Led to the development of electron microscopy (electrons have smaller λ → better resolution than light).
3. Verified by Davisson-Germer experiment (1927).
4. Foundation of quantum mechanics (Schrödinger's wave equation).

### 📝 Numerical Problems (Board Pattern)

**Num 1:** The work function of sodium is 2.3 eV. Find the threshold frequency and threshold wavelength.

**Solution:**
```
φ = 2.3 eV = 2.3 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 3.68×10⁻¹⁹ J

ν₀ = φ/h = 3.68×10⁻¹⁹ / 6.63×10⁻³⁴ = 5.55×10¹⁴ Hz

λ₀ = c/ν₀ = 3×10⁸ / 5.55×10¹⁴ = 5.4×10⁻⁷ m = 540 nm
```
**Answer: ν₀ = 5.55 × 10¹⁴ Hz, λ₀ = 540 nm (green light)**

---

**Num 2:** Light of wavelength 300 nm falls on a metal with work function 2.0 eV. Find: (a) energy of photon, (b) KE_max, (c) stopping potential.

**Solution:**
```
(a) E = hc/λ = (6.63×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸) / (300×10⁻⁹)
         = 6.63×10⁻¹⁹ J = 6.63×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ eV = 4.14 eV

(b) KE_max = E − φ = 4.14 − 2.0 = 2.14 eV
           = 2.14 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 3.42×10⁻¹⁹ J

(c) eV₀ = KE_max
    V₀ = 2.14 V
```
**Answer: (a) 4.14 eV, (b) 2.14 eV, (c) V₀ = 2.14 V**

---

**Num 3:** Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron moving with speed 2 × 10⁶ m/s.
(m = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg)

**Solution:**
```
λ = h/mv = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ / (9.1×10⁻³¹ × 2×10⁶)
  = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ / 18.2×10⁻²⁵
  = 0.364×10⁻⁹ m = 3.64 Å
```
**Answer: λ = 3.64 Å**

---

**Num 4:** An electron is accelerated through 100 V. Find its de Broglie wavelength.

**Solution:**
```
λ = 12.27/√V Å = 12.27/√100 = 12.27/10 = 1.227 Å
```
**Answer: λ = 1.227 Å**

---

**Num 5:** The stopping potential for a metal is 1.5 V when light of frequency 7.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz is used. Find the work function.

**Solution:**
```
eV₀ = hν − φ
φ = hν − eV₀
  = (6.63×10⁻³⁴ × 7.5×10¹⁴) − (1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 1.5)
  = 4.97×10⁻¹⁹ − 2.4×10⁻¹⁹
  = 2.57×10⁻¹⁹ J

In eV: φ = 2.57×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.61 eV
```
**Answer: φ = 1.61 eV**

---

**Num 6:** The de Broglie wavelength associated with a moving electron is 1.5 Å. Find its kinetic energy.

**Solution:**
```
λ = h/√(2m·KE)
KE = h²/(2mλ²)
   = (6.63×10⁻³⁴)² / (2 × 9.1×10⁻³¹ × (1.5×10⁻¹⁰)²)
   = 43.96×10⁻⁶⁸ / (2 × 9.1×10⁻³¹ × 2.25×10⁻²⁰)
   = 43.96×10⁻⁶⁸ / 40.95×10⁻⁵¹
   = 1.073×10⁻¹⁷ J

In eV: KE = 1.073×10⁻¹⁷ / 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 67 eV
```
**Answer: KE ≈ 67 eV**

---

**Num 7:** A photon and an electron have the same de Broglie wavelength of 2 Å. Compare their momenta and kinetic energies.

**Solution:**
```
Since λ = h/p → p = h/λ
Both have the SAME momentum (since same λ):
p_photon = p_electron = h/λ = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ / 2×10⁻¹⁰ = 3.315×10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s

KE of electron = p²/2m = (3.315×10⁻²⁴)² / (2 × 9.1×10⁻³¹)
               = 6.04×10⁻¹⁸ J = 37.7 eV

KE of photon = pc = 3.315×10⁻²⁴ × 3×10⁸ = 9.945×10⁻¹⁶ J = 6216 eV
```
**Answer: Same momentum; photon has ~165 times more energy than the electron.**

### 💡 Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Work function unit:** Always convert eV → Joules when using SI formula. 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
2. **Threshold frequency vs threshold wavelength:** ν₀ is MINIMUM frequency, λ₀ is MAXIMUM wavelength (they are inversely related).
3. **Stopping potential is INDEPENDENT of intensity** — intensity only changes the photocurrent (number of electrons), not their maximum energy.
4. **de Broglie wavelength formula:** λ = h/mv — don't confuse m (mass) with me (electron mass). Use correct mass for proton/neutron/alpha.
5. **Davisson-Germer numbers:** Remember 54 V, 50° angle, λ_theory = 1.67 Å, λ_exp = 1.65 Å.
6. **Photon rest mass = zero**, but photon HAS momentum (p = h/λ). These are not contradictory.
7. **Einstein's Nobel Prize was for photoelectric effect**, NOT relativity — this is a common trick MCQ.
8. **In the graph V₀ vs ν:** Slope = h/e (same for all metals), but x-intercept (ν₀) and y-intercept (−φ/e) differ for each metal.
