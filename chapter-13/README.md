## 2. Atomic Nucleus — Structure & Size

### 2.1 What is a Nucleus?

- The nucleus is the **tiny, dense core** of an atom located at its centre.
- It contains **protons** (+ve charge) and **neutrons** (no charge), collectively called **nucleons**.
- Electrons revolve around the nucleus.

**Key quantities:**
- **Atomic Number (Z):** Number of protons in the nucleus.
- **Mass Number (A):** Total number of nucleons (protons + neutrons).
- **Number of Neutrons (N):** N = A − Z

**Notation:**
```
  A
 Z X
```
Example: ¹²₆C means Carbon with Z = 6 (protons), A = 12 (total nucleons), N = 6 (neutrons).

### 2.2 Size of the Nucleus ⭐

The radius of a nucleus is given by the empirical formula:
```
R = R₀ × A^(1/3)
```
Where:
- `R` = radius of nucleus
- `R₀` = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m = **1.2 fm (femtometre)**
- `A` = mass number

> 💡 **1 fermi (fm) = 1 femtometre = 10⁻¹⁵ m**

**Key conclusion:** R ∝ A^(1/3), meaning **nuclear volume ∝ A** (proportional to mass number).

## 3. Atomic Mass & Nuclear Density

### 3.1 Atomic Mass Unit (u)

- Masses of atoms/nuclei are expressed in **atomic mass unit (u)**.
- **1 u = 1.66054 × 10⁻²⁷ kg**
- 1 u = (1/12) × mass of ¹²C atom = **931.5 MeV/c²**

**Masses of particles:**
| Particle | Symbol | Mass |
|---|---|---|
| Proton | p | 1.00728 u = 1.6726 × 10⁻²⁷ kg |
| Neutron | n | 1.00866 u = 1.6749 × 10⁻²⁷ kg |
| Electron | e | 0.000549 u = 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg |

### 3.2 Nuclear Density ⭐

Volume of nucleus:
```
V = (4/3)πR³ = (4/3)π(R₀)³A
```

Nuclear density:
```
ρ = Mass/Volume = A×mₙ / [(4/3)π R₀³ A]
  = mₙ / [(4/3)π R₀³]
```

**Nuclear density ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³**

> 📝 **Important conclusion:** Nuclear density is **independent of mass number A** and is the **same for all nuclei**. This is a very popular board question.

## 4. Isotopes, Isobars, and Isotones

### 4.1 Isotopes ⭐ (Board Exam Favourite)

**Definition:** *"Atoms of the same element having the same atomic number (Z) but different mass numbers (A) are called isotopes."*

- Same Z (same number of protons), different N (different neutrons).
- They have **same chemical properties** but different physical properties.

**Examples:**
```
¹H₁  ²H₁  ³H₁      (Hydrogen, Deuterium, Tritium — isotopes of Hydrogen)
 
¹²C₆  ¹³C₆  ¹⁴C₆   (Isotopes of Carbon)

²³⁵U₉₂  ²³⁸U₉₂     (Isotopes of Uranium — used in nuclear reactions!)
```

### 4.2 Isobars ⭐

**Definition:** *"Atoms of different elements having the same mass number (A) but different atomic numbers (Z) are called isobars."*

- Different Z, Same A.
- They are **different elements** so different chemical properties.

**Examples:**
```
¹⁴C₆  and  ¹⁴N₇       (Same A = 14, different Z)
⁴⁰Ca₂₀  and  ⁴⁰Ar₁₈   (Same A = 40, different Z)
```

### 4.3 Isotones ⭐

**Definition:** *"Atoms having the same number of neutrons (N) but different atomic numbers (Z) and mass numbers (A) are called isotones."*

- Same N = A − Z.

**Examples:**
```
¹⁴C₆  (N = 8)  and  ¹⁵N₇  (N = 8)   → Same N = 8
³H₁   (N = 2)  and  ⁴He₂  (N = 2)   → Same N = 2
```

### 4.4 Quick Comparison Table

| Term | Same | Different | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Isotopes** | Atomic number (Z) | Mass number (A) | ¹H, ²H, ³H |
| **Isobars** | Mass number (A) | Atomic number (Z) | ¹⁴C, ¹⁴N |
| **Isotones** | Neutron number (N) | Z and A | ¹⁴C, ¹⁵N |

> 📝 **Memory Tip:**
> - **Iso**topes → same **to**pic element (same Z)
> - **Iso**bars → same **bar** (same A = same mass bar)
> - **Iso**tones → same neu**tones** (neutron count)

## 5. Mass Defect and Binding Energy

### 5.1 Mass Defect ⭐⭐ (Very Important)

**Observation:** When we calculate the mass of a nucleus from its protons and neutrons, we find it is **less than the actual measured mass** of those individual particles. This difference is called **mass defect**.

**Definition:** *"The difference between the sum of masses of individual nucleons (protons + neutrons) and the actual mass of the nucleus is called mass defect."*

```
Δm = [Z×mₚ + (A−Z)×mₙ] − M
```
Where:
- `Δm` = mass defect
- `Z` = number of protons
- `A−Z` = number of neutrons
- `mₚ` = mass of one proton = 1.00728 u
- `mₙ` = mass of one neutron = 1.00866 u
- `M` = actual measured mass of nucleus

**Why does mass defect occur?** → Because some mass is converted into **binding energy** (which holds nucleons together). This is Einstein's famous mass-energy equivalence.

### 5.2 Einstein's Mass-Energy Equivalence ⭐⭐⭐

**Formula:**
```
E = mc²
```
Where:
- `E` = energy
- `m` = mass
- `c` = speed of light = 3 × 10⁸ m/s

**Conversion:**
```
1 u = 931.5 MeV
```
So: **Energy of 1 u mass = 931.5 MeV/c²**

> 💡 **MeV = Mega electron Volt = 10⁶ eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹³ J**

### 5.3 Binding Energy ⭐⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 3 or 5 Mark)

**Definition:** *"Binding energy is the energy required to completely separate all the nucleons of a nucleus from each other (i.e., break the nucleus into its constituent protons and neutrons)."*

OR equivalently:

*"Binding energy is the energy released when individual protons and neutrons come together to form a nucleus."*

**Formula:**
```
Eᵦ = Δm × c² = Δm × 931.5 MeV        (when Δm is in atomic mass units u)
```

**Worked Example:**

For Helium nucleus ⁴He₂:
- Z = 2, A = 4, N = 2
- mₚ = 1.00728 u, mₙ = 1.00866 u
- Actual mass of ⁴He = 4.00151 u

```
Sum of nucleon masses = 2 × 1.00728 + 2 × 1.00866
                      = 2.01456 + 2.01732 = 4.03188 u

Mass defect: Δm = 4.03188 − 4.00151 = 0.03037 u

Binding Energy = 0.03037 × 931.5 = 28.3 MeV
```

## 6. Binding Energy Per Nucleon & Stability

### 6.1 Binding Energy Per Nucleon ⭐⭐⭐ (Most Important Graph!)

**Definition:** *"Binding energy per nucleon is the average energy required to remove one nucleon from the nucleus."*

```
Eᵦ/A = Total Binding Energy / Total Number of Nucleons
```

### 6.2 Binding Energy Per Nucleon vs Mass Number Graph ⭐⭐⭐

This graph is the **most important concept** in this chapter. It is asked in almost every board exam.

```
Eᵦ/A
(MeV)
 |
9 |         ⁵⁶Fe (Iron — most stable!)
8 |      ___/________\___
7 |    /                 \
6 |   /  ⁴He               \
5 |  /                       \
4 | /²H                        \
3 |/                             \  ²³⁵U, ²³⁸U
2 |
1 |  ¹H
0 |_____________________________
  0   20  40  60  80 ... 200  240   A (Mass Number)
```

**Key observations from the graph:**

1. **Iron (⁵⁶Fe) has the highest Eᵦ/A ≈ 8.75 MeV** → Most stable nucleus.
2. For **light nuclei** (small A): Eᵦ/A increases sharply → **Fusion releases energy**.
3. For **heavy nuclei** (large A): Eᵦ/A decreases → **Fission releases energy**.
4. Middle region (A = 30 to 90): Most stable nuclei.
5. ⁴He, ¹²C, ¹⁶O have unusually high Eᵦ/A → exceptionally stable.

> 📝 **Board Exam Tip:** This graph is asked as a 3-mark question. Draw it properly with Fe at the peak, light nuclei on left, heavy nuclei on right. Explain fission and fusion from it.

### 6.3 How the Graph Explains Fission and Fusion

- **Fission (heavy → medium):** Uranium (low Eᵦ/A) splits → Iron-range fragments (higher Eᵦ/A). Energy released = difference in Eᵦ/A.
- **Fusion (light → medium):** Hydrogen (low Eᵦ/A) fuses → Helium (higher Eᵦ/A). Energy released = difference in Eᵦ/A.

## 7. Nuclear Forces

### 7.1 Definition ⭐

**Nuclear force** is the strong attractive force that holds protons and neutrons together inside the nucleus.

### 7.2 Properties of Nuclear Forces ⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 3 Mark)

1. **Short range:** Nuclear forces are effective only over a very short range of about **1–3 fm** (10⁻¹⁵ m). Beyond this, they become negligible.
2. **Strongest force in nature:** About **100 times stronger** than electromagnetic force.
3. **Charge independent:** Nuclear force between p–p, n–n, and p–n are almost equal (it doesn't depend on charge).
4. **Not a central force:** They depend on spin orientation of nucleons.
5. **Saturating nature:** A nucleon interacts only with its nearest neighbours, not all nucleons.
6. **No classical analogue:** Unlike gravity or electrostatic force, nuclear force has no classical equivalent.
7. **Repulsive at very short distances (<0.8 fm):** Prevents the nucleus from collapsing.

### 7.3 Nuclear Force vs Electrostatic Force

| Property | Nuclear Force | Electrostatic Force |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Attractive (short range) | Attractive or Repulsive |
| Range | Short (~1–3 fm) | Long range (∞) |
| Charge dependence | Independent of charge | Depends on charge |
| Strength | Very strong | Much weaker |
| At very small distance | Repulsive | Attractive (between unlike) |

## 8. Radioactivity

### 8.1 Discovery

**Radioactivity** was discovered by **Henri Becquerel** in 1896 while studying uranium salts.

**Marie Curie** and **Pierre Curie** later discovered radioactive elements **polonium (Po)** and **radium (Ra)**.

### 8.2 Definition ⭐

**Definition:** *"Radioactivity is the phenomenon in which unstable nuclei spontaneously disintegrate (decay) by emitting radiation in the form of alpha (α), beta (β), or gamma (γ) particles/rays, and in the process transform into more stable nuclei."*

### 8.3 Key Points

- It is a **spontaneous** process — cannot be controlled by external conditions (temperature, pressure, chemical state).
- It is a **random** process — we cannot predict which nucleus will decay next.
- It is a **nuclear phenomenon** — it originates from the nucleus, not the electron cloud.
- Obeys the **law of conservation of charge** and **mass number**.

### 8.4 Units of Radioactivity

| Unit | Symbol | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Becquerel (SI unit) | Bq | 1 disintegration per second |
| Curie | Ci | 3.7 × 10¹⁰ disintegrations per second |
| Rutherford | Rd | 10⁶ disintegrations per second |

```
1 Curie (Ci) = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq
```

## 9. Types of Radioactive Decay

### 9.1 Alpha (α) Decay ⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 2 Mark)

**What is emitted?** An **alpha particle** (⁴He₂ nucleus) = 2 protons + 2 neutrons.

**General equation:**
```
ᴬZX  →  ᴬ⁻⁴Z₋₂Y  +  ⁴₂He
```

**Rules:**
- Mass number decreases by **4**
- Atomic number decreases by **2**

**Example:**
```
²³⁸₉₂U  →  ²³⁴₉₀Th  +  ⁴₂He  +  energy (α particle)
```

**Why alpha decay?** Heavy nuclei (A > 82) have too many nucleons. Emitting an alpha particle reduces nuclear instability.

**Properties of α particles:**
- Charge: +2e
- Mass: 4u
- Range in air: ~5–10 cm
- Stopped by: A sheet of paper
- Ionizing power: Highest among α, β, γ

### 9.2 Beta (β) Decay ⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 2 Mark)

**Two types of beta decay:**

#### (a) Beta-minus (β⁻) decay:
A **neutron** converts to a **proton** + electron + antineutrino.
```
n  →  p  +  e⁻  +  ν̄ₑ  (antineutrino)
```

**General equation:**
```
ᴬZX  →  ᴬZ₊₁Y  +  ⁻¹₀e  +  ν̄ₑ
```

**Rules:**
- Mass number: **unchanged** (A stays same)
- Atomic number: **increases by 1**

**Example:**
```
¹⁴₆C  →  ¹⁴₇N  +  e⁻  +  ν̄ₑ      (used in carbon dating!)
³²₁₅P  →  ³²₁₆S  +  e⁻  +  ν̄ₑ
```

#### (b) Beta-plus (β⁺) decay:
A **proton** converts to a **neutron** + positron + neutrino.
```
p  →  n  +  e⁺  +  νₑ  (neutrino)
```

**General equation:**
```
ᴬZX  →  ᴬZ₋₁Y  +  ⁺¹₀e  +  νₑ
```

**Example:**
```
²²₁₁Na  →  ²²₁₀Ne  +  e⁺  +  νₑ
```

**Properties of β particles:**
- Charge: −1e (β⁻) or +1e (β⁺)
- Mass: electron mass
- Range in air: ~1 metre
- Stopped by: 3–4 mm of aluminium
- Ionizing power: Medium

> 📝 **Important:** **Neutrino (νₑ)** and **Antineutrino (ν̄ₑ)** were proposed by **Pauli** to explain the continuous energy spectrum of β particles and conserve energy and momentum in β decay.

### 9.3 Gamma (γ) Decay ⭐⭐

**What is emitted?** A **gamma ray photon** (high-energy electromagnetic radiation).

**When does it occur?** After α or β decay, the daughter nucleus is often left in an excited state. It comes to ground state by emitting a γ photon.

**General equation:**
```
ᴬZ X*  →  ᴬZX  +  γ
```

(Note: Z and A do NOT change in γ decay — only energy changes)

**Example:**
```
²³⁴₉₀Th*  →  ²³⁴₉₀Th  +  γ      (after previous α decay, excited Th emits γ)
```

**Properties of γ rays:**
- Charge: 0 (neutral)
- Mass: 0
- Speed: c (speed of light)
- Range in air: Very large (several metres)
- Stopped by: Several cm of lead or concrete
- Ionizing power: Lowest (but most penetrating!)

### 9.4 Comparison of α, β, γ

| Property | α particle | β particle | γ ray |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Helium nucleus | Electron/Positron | EM radiation (photon) |
| Charge | +2e | −e or +e | 0 |
| Mass | 4u | ~0 | 0 |
| Speed | ~0.1c | Up to 0.9c | c |
| Range in air | ~5–10 cm | ~1 m | Very large |
| Stopped by | Paper | Aluminium (few mm) | Lead (several cm) |
| Ionizing power | **Maximum** | Medium | **Minimum** |
| Penetrating power | **Minimum** | Medium | **Maximum** |
| Change in A | −4 | 0 | 0 |
| Change in Z | −2 | ±1 | 0 |

## 10. Radioactive Decay Law

### 10.1 Statement ⭐⭐⭐ (Most Important Derivation!)

**Statement:** *"The rate of disintegration of a radioactive sample at any instant is directly proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present in the sample at that instant."*

### 10.2 Mathematical Derivation ⭐⭐⭐ (5 Mark — Must Learn!)

Let:
- `N` = number of undecayed nuclei at time `t`
- `dN/dt` = rate of disintegration (decay rate)

According to the law:
```
dN/dt  ∝  −N

dN/dt  =  −λN        ...(1)
```
Where `λ` = **decay constant** (probability of decay per nucleus per second). The negative sign indicates N is decreasing.

**Solving the differential equation:**

Rearranging equation (1):
```
dN/N = −λ dt
```

Integrating both sides:
```
∫ dN/N = −λ ∫ dt

ln N = −λt + C        ...(2)
```

At t = 0, N = N₀ (initial number of nuclei):
```
ln N₀ = C
```

Substituting in (2):
```
ln N − ln N₀ = −λt

ln(N/N₀) = −λt
```

Taking exponential:
```
N = N₀ e^(−λt)
```

**This is the Radioactive Decay Law (Exponential Decay).**

### 10.3 Activity (Rate of Decay) ⭐⭐

**Activity (A):** Number of disintegrations per second.
```
A = |dN/dt| = λN

A = λN₀ e^(−λt) = A₀ e^(−λt)
```

Where `A₀ = λN₀` = initial activity.

**SI Unit of Activity:** Becquerel (Bq) = 1 decay per second.

## 11. Half-Life and Mean Life

### 11.1 Half-Life (T₁/₂) ⭐⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 3 Mark)

**Definition:** *"Half-life of a radioactive substance is the time in which half of the nuclei present initially decay (i.e., the number of undecayed nuclei becomes half)."*

**Derivation:**

At t = T₁/₂, N = N₀/2. Substituting in the decay equation:
```
N₀/2 = N₀ e^(−λ T₁/₂)

1/2 = e^(−λ T₁/₂)

Taking natural log:
ln(1/2) = −λ T₁/₂

−ln2 = −λ T₁/₂

T₁/₂ = ln2 / λ = 0.693 / λ
```

**Formula:**
```
T₁/₂ = 0.693 / λ
```

**Relationship between N and T₁/₂:**

After `n` half-lives (t = nT₁/₂):
```
N = N₀ × (1/2)ⁿ
```

| Number of Half-lives (n) | Fraction remaining (N/N₀) | % remaining |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | 100% |
| 1 | 1/2 | 50% |
| 2 | 1/4 | 25% |
| 3 | 1/8 | 12.5% |
| 4 | 1/16 | 6.25% |
| 10 | 1/1024 | ~0.1% |

**Examples of Half-Lives:**
| Element | Half-Life |
|---|---|
| ²³⁸U (Uranium) | 4.5 × 10⁹ years |
| ¹⁴C (Carbon-14) | 5730 years |
| ²²⁶Ra (Radium) | 1600 years |
| ¹³¹I (Iodine) | 8 days |
| ²²²Rn (Radon) | 3.8 days |
| ²³⁵U (Uranium) | 7 × 10⁸ years |

### 11.2 Mean Life (τ) ⭐⭐

**Definition:** *"Mean life (or average life) of a radioactive substance is the average time spent by a nucleus before disintegrating."*

**Derivation result:**
```
τ = 1/λ
```

**Relationship between T₁/₂ and τ:**
```
τ = T₁/₂ / 0.693 = 1.443 × T₁/₂

τ > T₁/₂ (mean life is always greater than half-life)
```

### 11.3 Connecting All Three: λ, T₁/₂, τ

```
λ = 0.693 / T₁/₂  =  1/τ

T₁/₂ = 0.693/λ = 0.693 τ

τ = 1/λ = T₁/₂ / 0.693 = 1.443 T₁/₂
```

> 📝 **Board Exam Tip:** The derivation of T₁/₂ from the decay law is asked as a 3-5 mark question. Write all steps clearly.

## 12. Nuclear Reactions

### 12.1 What is a Nuclear Reaction?

A nuclear reaction occurs when the **nucleus of an atom interacts** with another nucleus or particle and undergoes a transformation.

**General notation:**
```
Target + Projectile → Products + Energy
a + X  →  Y + b  (written as X(a, b)Y)
```

### 12.2 Laws Conserved in Nuclear Reactions ⭐

1. **Conservation of Charge:** Sum of atomic numbers (Z) must be equal on both sides.
2. **Conservation of Mass Number:** Sum of mass numbers (A) must be equal on both sides.
3. **Conservation of Energy:** Total energy (including rest mass energy) is conserved.
4. **Conservation of Linear Momentum**
5. **Conservation of Angular Momentum**

### 12.3 Q-Value of Nuclear Reaction ⭐⭐

**Definition:** *"The energy released or absorbed in a nuclear reaction is called the Q-value."*

```
Q = (Mass of reactants − Mass of products) × c²
Q = Δm × 931.5 MeV       (when Δm is in u)
```

- If Q > 0: **Exothermic** reaction (energy released) — spontaneous
- If Q < 0: **Endothermic** reaction (energy absorbed) — non-spontaneous

## 13. Nuclear Fission

### 13.1 Definition ⭐⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 3 or 5 Mark)

**Definition:** *"Nuclear fission is the process in which a heavy nucleus (like Uranium or Plutonium) splits into two or more lighter nuclei of comparable masses, accompanied by the release of a large amount of energy and a few neutrons."*

### 13.2 Discovery

Nuclear fission was discovered by **Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann** in 1939.

**Explained theoretically** by **Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch**.

### 13.3 Fission of Uranium-235 ⭐⭐⭐ (Most Important Example)

When a slow (thermal) neutron is captured by ²³⁵U:

```
²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n  →  ²³⁶₉₂U*  →  ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3¹₀n + Energy (200 MeV)
```

OR another possible reaction:
```
²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n  →  ¹³³₅₁Sb + ⁹⁹₄₁Nb + 4¹₀n + Energy
```

**Energy released per fission ≈ 200 MeV** (this is enormous compared to chemical reactions which release a few eV!)

### 13.4 Chain Reaction ⭐⭐

The **3 neutrons released** in each fission can cause further fissions → those release more neutrons → more fissions... This is called a **chain reaction**.

```
1 fission → 3 neutrons
3 neutrons → 3 more fissions → 9 neutrons
9 neutrons → 9 fissions → 27 neutrons
... (exponential growth)
```

**Types of chain reaction:**
- **Uncontrolled chain reaction:** Explosive release of energy → **Atom Bomb (Nuclear Bomb)**
- **Controlled chain reaction:** Slow, sustained release → **Nuclear Reactor (for electricity)**

### 13.5 Critical Mass ⭐

**Critical mass** is the minimum mass of fissile material needed to sustain a chain reaction.

- Below critical mass: Chain reaction dies out.
- At critical mass: Steady, sustained chain reaction (reactor condition).
- Above critical mass: Explosive uncontrolled reaction (bomb condition).

### 13.6 Nuclear Reactor ⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 3 Mark)

A nuclear reactor controls the fission chain reaction to produce electricity.

**Key components:**

| Component | Material | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Fuel** | ²³⁵U or ²³⁹Pu | Fissile material that undergoes fission |
| **Moderator** | Heavy water (D₂O), Graphite | Slows down fast neutrons to thermal speed |
| **Control Rods** | Boron or Cadmium | Absorb excess neutrons, control reaction rate |
| **Coolant** | Water, CO₂, liquid sodium | Removes heat from reactor core |
| **Shield** | Thick concrete | Absorbs harmful radiation |

**Working principle:**
1. ²³⁵U fission releases fast neutrons + heat.
2. Moderator slows neutrons down (makes them suitable for further fission).
3. Control rods absorb excess neutrons to prevent uncontrolled reaction.
4. Heat is used to generate steam → drives turbines → generates electricity.

## 14. Nuclear Fusion

### 14.1 Definition ⭐⭐⭐ (Board Exam — 3 Mark)

**Definition:** *"Nuclear fusion is the process in which two or more light nuclei combine together to form a heavier nucleus, with the release of a tremendous amount of energy."*

### 14.2 Proton-Proton (pp) Chain in the Sun ⭐⭐⭐

The energy of the sun (and other stars) comes from proton-proton fusion:

**Step 1:**
```
¹₁H + ¹₁H  →  ²₁H + e⁺ + νₑ        (Positron + Neutrino emitted)
```

**Step 2:**
```
²₁H + ¹₁H  →  ³₂He + γ              (Gamma ray emitted)
```

**Step 3:**
```
³₂He + ³₂He  →  ⁴₂He + 2¹₁H         (Helium formed + 2 protons released)
```

**Net reaction:**
```
4 ¹₁H  →  ⁴₂He + 2e⁺ + 2νₑ + 6γ  +  26.7 MeV
```

**Energy released per fusion event ≈ 26.7 MeV**

### 14.3 Thermonuclear Fusion ⭐⭐

For fusion to occur, the two nuclei must come very close (within 10⁻¹⁵ m) to overcome the **electrostatic repulsion** between positively charged nuclei.

This requires extremely **high temperatures (10⁷ to 10⁸ K)** → called **thermonuclear reaction**.

**Hydrogen Bomb:** Uses uncontrolled fusion (triggered by a fission bomb to create the necessary temperature).

**Controlled Fusion (Future technology):** Scientists are working on **ITER (International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor)** to harness controlled fusion as a clean, near-limitless energy source.

### 14.4 Comparison: Fission vs Fusion ⭐⭐

| Property | Nuclear Fission | Nuclear Fusion |
|---|---|---|
| Process | Heavy nucleus splits | Light nuclei combine |
| Fuel | ²³⁵U, ²³⁹Pu | ²H (Deuterium), ³H (Tritium) |
| Energy per nucleon | Less (~1 MeV/nucleon) | More (~3.5 MeV/nucleon) |
| Temperature needed | Room temp (slow neutrons) | ~10⁷–10⁸ K |
| Radioactive waste | Large amount (problem!) | Very little |
| Control | Possible (nuclear reactor) | NOT YET fully controlled |
| Real world use | Nuclear power plants, Atom bomb | Hydrogen bomb, Sun's energy |
| Chain reaction | Yes | Not required |

## 16. Board Exam Questions with Answers

### 📝 1-Mark / Very Short Answer Questions

**Q1. What is radioactivity?**
**Ans:** Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of radiation (α, β, or γ) from an unstable nucleus.

**Q2. Who discovered radioactivity?**
**Ans:** Henri Becquerel in 1896.

**Q3. What is the SI unit of radioactivity?**
**Ans:** Becquerel (Bq). 1 Bq = 1 disintegration per second.

**Q4. Define half-life of a radioactive element.**
**Ans:** Half-life is the time in which half of the initial radioactive nuclei decay. T₁/₂ = 0.693/λ.

**Q5. What is a chain reaction?**
**Ans:** A self-sustaining nuclear reaction in which neutrons produced in fission cause further fissions is called a chain reaction.

**Q6. What is nuclear binding energy?**
**Ans:** Binding energy is the energy required to completely break a nucleus into its individual protons and neutrons. Eᵦ = Δm × c².

**Q7. What change occurs in Z and A during α decay?**
**Ans:** A decreases by 4, Z decreases by 2.

**Q8. What change occurs in Z and A during β⁻ decay?**
**Ans:** A remains unchanged, Z increases by 1.

**Q9. What change occurs in Z and A during γ decay?**
**Ans:** Neither A nor Z changes. Only energy is emitted.

**Q10. What is the energy equivalent of 1 atomic mass unit?**
**Ans:** 1 u = 931.5 MeV

**Q11. Define isotopes.**
**Ans:** Atoms of the same element with same atomic number (Z) but different mass numbers (A) are called isotopes. Example: ¹H, ²H, ³H.

**Q12. What is nuclear fission?**
**Ans:** The splitting of a heavy nucleus into lighter nuclei of comparable size, with release of energy and neutrons, is called nuclear fission.

**Q13. What is the moderator in a nuclear reactor?**
**Ans:** Moderator slows down fast neutrons to thermal speeds. Examples: heavy water (D₂O), graphite.

**Q14. What are isotopes, isobars, and isotones? Give one example of each.**

**Ans:**
- **Isotopes:** Same Z, different A. Example: ¹H, ²H (Hydrogen and Deuterium)
- **Isobars:** Same A, different Z. Example: ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N
- **Isotones:** Same neutron number N. Example: ¹⁴C (N=8) and ¹⁵N (N=8)

**Ans:**
Mass defect is the difference between the sum of masses of individual nucleons and the actual mass of the nucleus.
```
Δm = [Zmₚ + (A−Z)mₙ] − M
Binding Energy = Δm × 931.5 MeV
```

**Q16. Write the properties of nuclear forces. (Any 4)**

**Ans:**
1. They are short-range forces (effective only up to ~1–3 fm).
2. They are the strongest forces in nature.
3. They are charge-independent (same between p-p, n-n, p-n pairs).
4. They are attractive in nature but become repulsive at very short distances (<0.8 fm).

**Ans:**
| Fission | Fusion |
|---|---|
| Heavy nucleus splits into lighter ones | Light nuclei combine to form heavier one |
| Fuel: ²³⁵U or ²³⁹Pu | Fuel: Deuterium, Tritium |
| Produces radioactive waste | Produces very little waste |
| Occurs at room temperature | Requires ~10⁷–10⁸ K |

**Q18. What is meant by the decay constant? How is it related to half-life?**

**Ans:** The decay constant (λ) is the probability of disintegration of a nucleus per unit time. Its SI unit is s⁻¹.

Relation with half-life:
```
T₁/₂ = 0.693/λ    or equivalently    λ = 0.693/T₁/₂
```

### 📝 3-Mark Questions

**Q19. Derive the expression for the half-life of a radioactive substance.**

**Ans:**

From the radioactive decay law: N = N₀ e^(−λt)

At t = T₁/₂, by definition, N = N₀/2:

```
N₀/2 = N₀ e^(−λ T₁/₂)

1/2 = e^(−λ T₁/₂)

Taking natural log on both sides:
ln(1/2) = −λ T₁/₂
−0.693 = −λ T₁/₂

T₁/₂ = 0.693/λ
```

### 📝 Numerical Problems (Board Pattern)

**Num 1:** A radioactive substance has a half-life of 5 years. What fraction remains after 20 years?

**Solution:**
```
n = Total time / T₁/₂ = 20/5 = 4 half-lives

Fraction remaining = (1/2)ⁿ = (1/2)⁴ = 1/16
```
**Answer: 1/16 (or 6.25%) of the original amount remains.**

---

**Num 2:** The decay constant of a radioactive element is 1.386 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹. Find its half-life and mean life.

**Solution:**
```
T₁/₂ = 0.693/λ = 0.693 / (1.386×10⁻³)
      = 500 s

τ = 1/λ = 1 / (1.386×10⁻³) = 721.5 s
```
**Answer: T₁/₂ = 500 s, τ ≈ 721.5 s**

---

**Num 3:** Calculate the binding energy of ¹⁶O nucleus.
(Given: m(¹⁶O) = 15.9949 u, mₚ = 1.00728 u, mₙ = 1.00866 u)

**Solution:**
```
Z = 8, N = 8 for ¹⁶O

Sum of nucleon masses = 8×1.00728 + 8×1.00866
                     = 8.05824 + 8.06928 = 16.12752 u

Mass defect: Δm = 16.12752 − 15.9949 = 0.13262 u

Eᵦ = 0.13262 × 931.5 = 123.5 MeV

Eᵦ/nucleon = 123.5/16 = 7.72 MeV/nucleon
```
**Answer: Binding energy = 123.5 MeV, Eᵦ/A = 7.72 MeV/nucleon**

---

**Num 4:** The activity of a radioactive sample is 3700 disintegrations/second at t = 0. After 3 hours, it reduces to 185 disintegrations/second. Find the half-life.

**Solution:**
```
A = A₀ e^(−λt)
185 = 3700 × e^(−λ × 3×3600)

185/3700 = e^(−10800λ)
1/20 = e^(−10800λ)

ln(1/20) = −10800λ
−2.996 = −10800λ
λ = 2.996/10800 = 2.774 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹

T₁/₂ = 0.693/λ = 0.693/(2.774×10⁻⁴)
      = 2498 s ≈ 2500 s ≈ 41.7 minutes
```
**Answer: T₁/₂ ≈ 41.7 minutes**

---

**Num 5:** What is the nuclear radius of ²⁷Al? (R₀ = 1.2 fm)

**Solution:**
```
R = R₀ × A^(1/3)
  = 1.2 × (27)^(1/3)
  = 1.2 × 3
  = 3.6 fm
```
**Answer: R = 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ m**

---

**Num 6:** A sample initially has N₀ = 6.02 × 10²³ atoms of a radioactive element with T₁/₂ = 1600 years. Find the initial activity.

**Solution:**
```
λ = 0.693/T₁/₂ = 0.693 / (1600 × 3.15×10⁷)

= 0.693 / (5.04×10¹⁰) = 1.375×10⁻¹¹ s⁻¹

A₀ = λN₀ = 1.375×10⁻¹¹ × 6.02×10²³
   = 8.28×10¹² Bq
```
**Answer: A₀ ≈ 8.28 × 10¹² Bq (this corresponds to ~224 Curie)**

### 💡 Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Don't confuse T₁/₂ and τ.** Mean life τ = 1.443 × T₁/₂. Mean life is ALWAYS greater than half-life.
2. **In β⁻ decay:** A stays SAME, Z increases by 1. Students often write A decreasing.
3. **In γ decay:** Neither A nor Z changes. It's only energy emission.
4. **Binding energy per nucleon formula:** Eᵦ/A = Total binding energy ÷ Mass number A.
5. **Nuclear density is SAME for all nuclei** — do not say it increases with A.
6. **Fusion needs extremely high temperature** (~10⁷ K) — do not say it happens at room temp. Fission (with slow neutrons) can occur at normal conditions.
7. **Control rods vs Moderator:** Control rods ABSORB neutrons (Boron/Cadmium). Moderator SLOWS neutrons (D₂O/Graphite). These are different things!
8. **Mass number A = protons + neutrons.** Atomic number Z = protons only. Neutrons N = A − Z.
9. **Do not confuse Curie and Becquerel.** 1 Ci = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq.
10. **Chain reaction in a bomb = uncontrolled. In a reactor = controlled.** This distinction is often asked.
